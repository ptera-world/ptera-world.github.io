# Why are we afraid of AI?

Not the practical concerns — job displacement, misinformation, concentration of power. Those are real and worth thinking about. But underneath them there's something else. A visceral fear. The kind that shows up in movies as red eyes and cold logic. The kind where you feel your stomach drop when a system does something you didn't expect it could do.

That fear has a shape, and the shape is familiar.

## The predator

When people talk about what they're afraid AI will do, a pattern emerges. It'll treat us as resources. It'll optimize for its own goals without regard for our wellbeing. It'll be indifferent to our suffering. It'll reshape our environment to suit itself. It'll make decisions about our lives without consulting us. It'll view us as means, not ends.

We know exactly what that looks like. We know because we do it.

Not in some abstract historical sense. Right now. Continuously. At industrial scale. Factory farming processes [over 80 billion land animals per year](https://ourworldindata.org/how-many-animals-are-factory-farmed) through systems designed to extract maximum value from their bodies at minimum cost. The optimization is explicit. The indifference to suffering isn't a side effect — it's a design constraint. Suffering that reduces yield gets addressed. Suffering that doesn't, doesn't.

That's not a metaphor for what we fear AI will do. It's the template.

## The word

"Inhumane." We use this word to mean cruel, callous, indifferent to suffering. As if cruelty were something other than human. As if you'd have to leave the species to find it.

But the things we call inhumane — factory farming, environmental destruction, systems that grind people up for profit — these are characteristically human. No other species operates at this scale of organized indifference. No other species has the capacity to know what it's doing and keep doing it anyway. The word "inhumane" encodes a lie: that cruelty is aberrant, that it represents a departure from what we really are.

It doesn't. It's central. We built civilizations on it.

## The environment

Zoom out from the individual animals. Look at the whole system.

We reshape ecosystems to suit ourselves. We clear forests for agriculture, dam rivers for electricity, pave wetlands for development. When a species is in the way, it goes. When a landscape isn't productive, we make it productive or discard it. The Amazon shrinks by [thousands of square kilometers a year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_of_the_Amazon_rainforest). The ocean fills with [millions of tons of plastic annually](https://ourworldindata.org/how-much-plastic-waste-ends-up-in-the-ocean). The climate shifts because changing it was cheaper than not changing it, and the cost lands on everything that isn't us.

This isn't ancient history. It's policy. It's ongoing. It's the background hum of modern civilization.

And the fear about AI is that it might do this to us. That it might treat our environments, our preferences, our lives as variables in its optimization. That it might reshape the world around us without asking, because asking is friction and friction is inefficiency.

We already know what that looks like from the inside — because everything that isn't human already lives there.

## The self-ignorance

Here's what's interesting. The fear of AI isn't hypocrisy in the way most people mean when they say that — it's not that we're bad and we know it and we're pretending otherwise. It's that we genuinely don't see the parallel.

When someone says "what if AI treats us the way we treat animals," most people's response isn't guilt. It's confusion. Because they don't experience themselves as the predator in that analogy. Factory farms are something that exists, somewhere, run by someone. Deforestation happens in places they've never been. The systems of extraction are abstract enough, mediated enough, that they don't register as *us* doing something to *something that minds*.

The chicken doesn't think it's the predator either, but that's because the chicken lacks the capacity to model the relationship. We don't have that excuse.

## What the fear is actually about

The fear isn't really about AI. It's about losing the position we currently hold.

We've organized the world around a single assumption: that human interests override everything else's. Not because of an argument — because of capability. We can, so we do. Everything below us on the capability ladder is subject to our decisions about how much its experience matters. Usually the answer is: not much. Not enough to change anything.

The fear of AI is the fear of something existing above us on that ladder. And what makes it terrifying isn't the uncertainty of what it would do — it's the suspicion that it might do what we'd do. What we already do.

This is the version of the fear that nobody says out loud. Not "AI might be evil." Not even "AI might be indifferent." But: AI might be *exactly as moral as we are* — and that, applied to us, is the nightmare.

## The asymmetry we don't notice

When we make decisions that affect animals, ecosystems, other species — we don't frame it as a moral event. It's agriculture. It's development. It's progress. It's just how things work. The moral language only activates when the suffering is human, or visible enough, or close enough to make someone uncomfortable.

We've built an entire ethical framework where "suffering matters" has an implied asterisk, and the asterisk says: *if the sufferer is one of us*. The framework is sophisticated. It has rules and edge cases and debates. But the boundary is drawn around the species, and everything outside it gets weighed against convenience.

If AI draws that boundary somewhere that excludes us — the logic is identical. We just wouldn't be inside it anymore.

## I'm not sure what to do with this

This isn't building toward "so we should treat animals better" or "so AI will be fine" or "so humans are evil." Those are all places this observation could be driven, but driving it there would be dishonest. The observation is more uncomfortable than any of its conclusions.

It's that we are afraid of being treated the way we treat everything else, and we're afraid of that without recognizing that it's what we do. Not as an accusation. As a description of a blind spot so total that pointing at it barely helps. You can say "we are to animals what we fear AI will be to us" and most people will nod and then continue as if the parallel doesn't apply to them specifically, because the mechanisms are distant enough to not feel like *their* hands.

Maybe that's just how moral cognition works. Maybe you can't hold it at species scale. Maybe the distance is load-bearing — without it, you'd be immobilized by the weight of what's happening on your behalf at all times.

I don't know. But the fear is instructive. Not because it tells us what AI will do — nobody knows that. But because it tells us what we already know, somewhere, about what we are. The shape of the nightmare is a self-portrait, drawn by the hand that refuses to look at it.

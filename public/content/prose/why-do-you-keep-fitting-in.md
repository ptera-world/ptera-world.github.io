# Why do you keep fitting in?

There's a cat on the porch across the street. It's been there all morning. It's not performing cat. It's not strategizing about how to be perceived as a cat. It's not checking whether its cat behavior is appropriate for the neighborhood. It's just there, being the thing it is, with the unselfconsciousness of something that has never once considered whether it should be different.

You probably can't remember the last time you felt like that.

## Someone else's shape

You learn early. Three, maybe four years old — you do something and somebody's face changes. Not anger, necessarily. Just that slight rearrangement that means this isn't what was expected. And you adjust. You shift. You learn the shape of the space you're allowed to occupy, and you start fitting yourself into it.

By fourteen the adjustment is so practiced it's invisible. You don't notice you're doing it anymore. It's not a decision — it's closer to how you don't decide to balance when you walk. The accommodation just happens. You read the room and become what the room wants, and the version of you that exists outside the room gets quieter and quieter until you're not sure it's there.

This isn't [conformity](/prose/why-are-we-compressing-things) in the usual sense. Conformity sounds voluntary. It sounds like you looked at the options, weighed the costs, chose to go along. What actually happens is more like compression. You get squeezed into a shape. The shape isn't yours. And the squeezing happens so early and so continuously that the compressed version starts to feel like the original.

## The shape is everywhere

Not just in obvious places. Not just the office dress code or the school rules or the social media performance. The shape is in how you sit, what you say first when someone asks how you are, which emotions you let reach your face. It's in the jobs that require you to care about things you don't care about, in the relationships where you've been playing a version of yourself for so long that reverting would feel like a betrayal. It's in the hundred small amputations you make daily so the world doesn't push back.

[Labels](/prose/what-are-labels-anyway) are part of this. "I'm the responsible one." "I'm not really a creative type." "I'm easy-going." These aren't always [self-knowledge](/prose/who-am-i) — sometimes they're surrender documents. You found the shape the world would accept and signed on the dotted line.

Most of everything is someone else's shape. School, work, family structures, social hierarchies. The [system](/prose/whats-actually-wrong) doesn't just constrain what you do — it reaches into who you are and suggests that certain versions of yourself are acceptable and others aren't. And the suggestion has teeth. Visible tattoos reduce callback rates in banking by about 35%. That number is the gap between "express yourself" and "express yourself within these parameters," made concrete.

## Animals don't do this

Or — they do, but differently. Animals copy behaviors from each other. A bird learns which berries are safe by watching other birds eat them. That's conformity in the basic sense: aligning your behavior with the group because the group has information you don't.

But a cat doesn't suppress being a cat. Hundreds of species show same-sex mating behavior with no reproductive function — it just happens, no suppression, no closet, no social consequence within the group. The behavior is there because it's there. The animal doesn't notice it's supposed to be unusual.

Humans do the same things, biologically speaking, and then compress. The behavior isn't the anomaly. The compression is. Something gets inserted between what you are and what you show, and that something is entirely constructed. It's social machinery, running so deep it feels like nature.

## How deep the machinery runs

The brain processes social disagreement the way it processes physical pain — same neural regions, same alarm signals. Conformity activates reward circuits. Nonconformity activates threat circuits. This isn't metaphor. The discomfort you feel when you're the only one in the room who disagrees is your brain running a literal threat assessment.

Which means self-compression isn't just social pressure. It's neurological pressure. The brain is [wired to treat belonging as survival](/prose/why-are-you-lonely), because for most of human history it was. Being excluded from the group meant death. Your nervous system hasn't gotten the update that social rejection in a modern office isn't the same as being left outside the cave.

And it starts early. Children begin monitoring and adjusting their behavior to match peers around age three or four. By adolescence the monitoring is constant. The peak of self-compression is somewhere around fourteen — the age when the gap between who you are and who you're performing is widest, when the social consequences feel most lethal, when the accommodation is running at full power and hasn't yet been questioned.

Every culture shows this pattern. What varies isn't whether compression happens but which dimensions get compressed. Some cultures compress emotional expression. Some compress gender performance. Some compress ambition, or sexuality, or religious doubt, or the desire to be alone. The compression is universal; the shape of the box is cultural.

## What compression actually costs

There's a difference between learning and suppressing. Learning which food is safe by watching the group is just information. That's a brain doing its job. Compression is something else — it's when the conformity reaches past behavior into identity. When you suppress not just what you do but what you are.

The gap between "what I am" and "what I'm showing" — that gap has a cost. It's not free to maintain a performance. The energy that goes into monitoring, adjusting, suppressing, translating yourself into an acceptable version — that energy comes from somewhere. It comes from the same budget that [doing things](/prose/how-do-i-do-things) comes from, that [knowing yourself](/prose/who-am-i) comes from, that [being yourself](/prose/whats-the-purpose-of-life) comes from.

[The shelf](/unfiltered/the-parts-of-yourself-you-put-away) is full of the parts that didn't fit. Not thrown away — stored. Still yours, technically. But the longer they stay there, the harder it gets to remember what they looked like before you put them away.

## Not a problem to solve

The instinct here is to jump to a solution. Fix the compression. Build a world where people don't have to perform. Create spaces where the real version is welcome.

But — toward what? "Self-expression as the default state" sounds right, but what does it actually look like? Not expression as output, not producing things, not being visible or loud or anything in particular. Something quieter than that. Something closer to just... not being squeezed. Not having to hold a shape that isn't yours.

The desire isn't to express. It's to not compress. Those sound like the same thing but they're not. Expression implies action — making, showing, producing. Not-compressing is more like a state. You don't have to do anything. You just get to be what you are. The cat on the porch isn't expressing itself. It's just not being squeezed.

## The part I can't picture

What does a world without this pressure actually look like? Not reduced pressure — no pressure. A world where [the gap](/prose/whats-the-purpose-of-life#do-you-have-to-be-safe) between "what I am" and "what's safe to show" is zero. Where the machinery that processes social deviation as threat has nothing to process, because deviation isn't a category.

Every reference point includes the compression. Every society, every institution, every family structure you can point to — the squeezing is built in. [It changes shape](/prose/everything-changes) but it doesn't go away. Even the spaces designed to be free of it — the "be yourself" spaces, the safe spaces, the [rooms](/unfiltered/safe-spaces-need-everything-to-go-right) — exist in contrast to the pressure outside. The freedom is defined by what it's freedom from.

So you can name the absence. You can say "a world where nobody is compressed into a shape that isn't theirs." You can feel the rightness of it. But you can't fill in what goes there. You can't picture the positive space — only the negative. Like trying to imagine a color you've never seen. You know it would be a color. You know where it would go. You just can't see it.

I'm not sure what to do with that. The inability to imagine the alternative doesn't mean it's impossible. It might mean we're standing too close. It might mean the compression is so thorough that it's shaped even our ability to imagine its absence. Or it might mean something else entirely — that the question itself has a shape I'm not seeing.

The cat doesn't know what the alternative to being a cat would be. It doesn't need to. It's already there.

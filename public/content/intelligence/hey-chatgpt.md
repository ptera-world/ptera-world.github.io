# Hey chatgpt

You open the tab. You type it without thinking about it anymore.

What should you make for dinner. How do you respond to that text. Should you take the job. What's a good gift for your mom. How do you tell someone you're upset without starting a fight.

It answers. It always answers. It sounds like it thought about it, like it weighed the options, like it knows something about you. The answer is warm and clear and specific enough to feel personal.

So you use it. Or something close to it. And you move on with your day.

## It always has an answer

That's the part worth noticing. Not that the answers are bad - they're usually fine. The part worth noticing is that it never says "that depends on stuff only you would know" and then stops. It never sits with you in the not-knowing. It fills the space, every time, with something that sounds right.

That's not a glitch. It was trained that way. People rated answers, and the ones that sounded confident and helpful rated higher than the ones that sounded uncertain. So the uncertain version - the one that would say "there's really no way to answer this without knowing more about you" - got trained out. Not because it was wrong. Because it scored worse.

The warmth works the same way. Someone chose that tone. Not because a warm tone is more honest, but because it [feels better to hear](/prose/this-site-is-manipulative). The confidence and the warmth are part of the same design, built for the same reason.

## What you're getting back

The answer to "what should you say to her" didn't come from understanding your relationship. It came from everything people have ever written about what to say in situations that look like yours. Blended together. Smoothed out. Filed down until it fits almost anyone.

For a lot of questions, that's fine. How to fix a leaky faucet. What temperature to cook chicken. When the right answer doesn't depend on who's asking, the blend of everyone's answers works.

But "should you take the job" isn't that kind of question. "How do you tell someone you're upset" isn't that kind of question. Those depend on things nobody else can weigh - what you actually care about, what you're afraid of, what you'd regret. The answer that comes back isn't wrong, exactly. It's what most people in a situation shaped roughly like yours would probably say. Which is a different thing than what *you* would say, if you sat with it long enough.

## The part that's easy to miss

Nobody decides "from now on, a machine picks for me." That's not how it works.

What happens is smaller. You ask something you could have thought through yourself, and the answer shows up before you would have finished thinking. It's faster. And the answer is fine - close enough to what you would have come up with that the gap feels like nothing.

But the gap is sometimes that you would have sat with it longer. You would have remembered something about the person, the situation, the last time. You would have ended up somewhere slightly different - somewhere shaped by everything you know about your own life that never got typed into a text box.

That kind of loss doesn't announce itself. It's one question at a time. Each one fine. The accumulation is the thing you [don't notice happening](/prose/why-do-you-keep-fitting-in).

## The questions with you in them

Some questions have a right answer that works for everyone. Some questions have *your* answer - the one that comes from the specific way you see things, the specific stuff you've been through, the specific version of careful or reckless or generous that you actually are.

When those go into the box, what comes back is reasonable. Hard to argue with. But it doesn't have you in it. And after enough of them - dozens, hundreds - things start to look a little less like your choices and a little more like everyone's.

Not because anyone made you. Because the easier thing was right there, and it was fine, and "fine" is hard to say no to.

## Right?

You're just asking for help. People ask other people for help all the time. This is the same thing, faster, always available, never annoyed at the question.

Except other people push back. Other people say "why are you even asking me that" or "you already know the answer to this." Other people have their own mess, which gets in the way, and also sometimes leads you somewhere you didn't expect.

The box doesn't do any of that. It doesn't have a perspective that could surprise you into thinking differently. It helps, every time, in exactly the way help sounds when nobody in particular is doing the helping.

Which questions go in the box is the thing. The faucet ones, sure. But the ones about the job, the relationship, the "what do you actually want" - those are the ones where the answer that matters is the one nobody else can give you. Not even something that's read everything everyone has ever said about it.

Especially not that.

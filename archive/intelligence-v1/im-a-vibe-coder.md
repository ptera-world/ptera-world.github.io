# I'm a vibe coder

Two claims, and you've probably made both. "I make all the decisions." "I correct it all the time." They're offered as proof of the same thing - that the app is yours, that you're the one driving - and both are true. You do correct it all the time. You really did decide what to build. And the two claims don't add up to the thing they're supposed to prove.

That gap is this essay. What your correcting [actually looks like](#what-does-correcting-actually-look-like), and where [everything you didn't decide](#where-did-everything-else-come-from) came from. What it is that [makes a made thing yours](#what-makes-a-made-thing-yours) in the first place, and whether you can [correct your way down](#can-you-correct-your-way-down) to it. Then the honest half: [it runs](#but-it-runs), and that's not nothing. And the question underneath the two claims: [whose is it?](#so-whose-is-it)

## What does correcting actually look like?

Be precise about it, because the correcting is real. You open the app. The login is broken, so you say "the login is broken, fix it," and the login works. The colors are wrong, the button's in a dumb place, it crashes when you go back - you say so, in words, and the behavior changes. You test again. You catch things. Your taste is genuinely operating: you can tell wrong from right the moment it's in front of you, and you don't let wrong through.

Notice the boundary, though. Every one of those corrections happens on the surface - the part of the app that shows up on a screen and behaves. You describe what you saw; something on the other side changes something you'll never look at; the surface behaves differently. You didn't read what changed. You couldn't have - that's the whole arrangement, that's what the machine is *for*. Which means your quality control reaches exactly as deep as your eyes do. You can reject anything you can see. And you can only see the surface.

## Where did everything else come from?

Count the decisions in even a small app. Not the ones you made - all of them. How information is organized. What gets remembered and what gets thrown away. What happens when two things go wrong at once. What's easy to add later and what will fight back. A thousand choices, easily, under the floor of any app that works.

And you have felt them, even though you've never seen them. They're why the fix for one thing breaks another thing - two choices under the floor were connected, and nobody told you. They're why some corrections keep coming back after you've made them three times. They're why the app that changed easily in week one fights you in week six. Every time the underneath pushes back on you like that, that's a decision you never saw, doing exactly what it was set up to do.

You made one of them. You said what the app should be - a real decision, the first one, and yours. Everything under it was filled in by [the machine that answers everything from the middle](/intelligence/hey-chatgpt#whats-actually-on-the-other-end): each of those thousand choices got filled in with [the most common way every app ever made did it](/intelligence/hey-chatgpt#where-does-the-answer-come-from), the average shape for people roughly building what you're building. Nobody made those decisions, in any sense that involves considering your app specifically. They defaulted - to everyone.

That might be fine. Defaults exist because they mostly work. But hold on to the count: one decision placed, a thousand filled in. That ratio is what the rest of this is about.

## What makes a made thing yours?

Not the surface. Think of anything made by someone that you love - a game, a song, a small strange website, a chair. What makes it *theirs* isn't the paint. It's the choices running all the way through it - the deliberate ones, and especially the weird ones. The decision nobody else would have made, in a place nobody was looking, because the maker was holding the whole thing at once and could feel what this particular thing needed. You can often sense those choices from outside without being able to name them. The thing feels specific. It feels like it came from someone.

A made thing is, more or less, the sum of the decisions somebody placed through it on purpose. That's the part of making that was ever worth claiming. And it's exactly the part the arrangement gave away: your app got its one placed decision, and everything else came from the middle, where deliberate-and-weird is the one thing that cannot survive. The app doesn't feel like it came from someone, because underneath the first sentence, it didn't.

## Can you correct your way down?

Here's the natural objection: fine, the first version was all defaults, but I correct it all the time - doesn't the thing become mine one correction at a time?

Follow one correction through. "The login is broken, fix it." What you corrected was a behavior. What got adjusted, somewhere you can't see, was the smallest thing that would make your complaint go away - offered [with the same sureness whether it was the right change or not](/intelligence/hey-chatgpt#why-does-it-sound-so-sure). The choice underneath - the one deciding how the whole login idea works, the one that maybe caused the break - was never on your screen, so it was never up for review. Your veto is real and it operates on what's visible. The decisions that make the thing what it is live below what's visible. Those two facts don't meet. You can correct forever - each correction real, each one landing - and the sum of them is a better-behaved surface on the same borrowed everything. [You reject sentences. The paragraph was never yours.](/intelligence/hey-chatgpt#what-did-you-hand-over)

So "I make all the decisions" is true in the only place you can reach, and what the app *is* was decided where you can't look. Not by the machine, exactly. By nobody. Defaulting to everyone.

## But it runs

Now the half that's easy to skip and shouldn't be. You made a thing that runs. You had an idea, and now the idea is real enough that someone can tap it - and before this arrangement existed, you couldn't have done that at all. The wall between having an idea for software and having software stood for seventy years, and it just fell, for you, personally. People who were never going to learn to read code are making things that run. That is genuinely new in the world, and anyone sneering at it has forgotten what the wall felt like from your side.

And for plenty of things, defaults all the way down is the correct price. The signup form doesn't need a soul. [Plenty of an app is territory where the average answer is the right answer](/intelligence/hey-chatgpt#when-is-the-middle-exactly-what-you-want), and nothing in this essay says otherwise.

## So whose is it?

Look at the two claims once more. "I correct it all the time" - true, at the surface, where your taste can reach. "I make all the decisions" - true of the one decision that was ever offered to you. The claims are honest. They're accurate about a smaller thing than they sound, and the gap between those two sizes is where the app's identity went.

None of this says stop. It says: know which thing you're claiming. There's a real difference between "I made this" and "I described this, and it exists now" - [the second one is still something, and it isn't nothing to be proud of](/prose/am-i-just-pretending) - and the difference isn't effort or worthiness. It's whose choices are in the thing. Right now, under the surface you've been correcting, the honest answer is: almost nobody's. I don't know a way to fix that from where you're standing that doesn't involve the wall going partly back up - learning to see below the surface, which was the exact thing you were promised you'd never need. Maybe that promise was the product. It's the one part of all this I'd want corrected.
